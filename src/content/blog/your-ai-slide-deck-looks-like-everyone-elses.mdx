I asked AI to turn my idea and narrative into a slide deck. It gave me four
pillars, three precise-looking numbers, a move-now-or-fall-behind slide, rounded
cards, bright gradients, and a closing invitation to "build the future,
together."

  caption="Three feels incomplete. Five will not fit without making the font smaller."
/>

A bank, a software company, a consultancy, or a dog-food subscription service
could use it after changing the logo.

The familiar structure looks professional, but it also makes the deck
interchangeable.

LLMs are very good at producing plausible continuations. Ask for a corporate
deck and the likely continuation is familiar:

1. Vision
2. Current challenges
3. Three or four pillars
4. A simple process
5. Large numbers
6. A horizontal roadmap
7. "Why now?"
8. Inspirational close

That sameness is not only my impression. In a 2026 *PNAS Nexus* study,
[Emily Wenger and Yoed Kenett](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgag042)
gave standardized creativity tasks to many humans and multiple LLMs. Responses
from different LLMs resembled one another far more than responses from
different humans, even after the researchers controlled for key confounds. The
study did not test slide decks, but it supports the mechanism behind the
feeling: different models can still pull people toward similar creative
starting points.

Two preregistered experiments with 1,100 participants at CHI 2025 found a
related effect. [LLM assistance improved performance while it was
available](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3714198), but it did not improve
later unassisted creativity. In some conditions, originality and idea diversity
fell. More generated options did not automatically produce more original
thinking.

Presentation generators combine that familiar story with a small set of
familiar components: cards, circles, gradients, icons, timelines, and big
numbers.

The visual polish makes the problem worse. People tend to challenge a messy
draft. A polished deck can move through a review because it already looks
professional and finished.

## Generic work weakens the message

What should the audience remember or do? Which claim should they believe, and
what evidence supports it?

Generic layouts flatten those decisions. Every pillar receives the same card.
Every benefit gets the same icon. Every metric gets the same giant type.

- **Nothing sticks.** Familiar components produce recognition, not memory.
- **Your brand becomes replaceable.** If a competitor can publish the deck
  unchanged, the deck is not doing brand work.
- **The audience cannot find your point of view.** The slides show a category
  consensus instead of what you believe.
- **Decoration competes with evidence.** A gradient gets more space than the
  source behind a claim.
- **False precision borrows authority.** "42%" looks measured even when no
  measurement exists.

Three communication books give those failures practical names:

- Chip and Dan Heath's [*Made to
  Stick*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/77687/made-to-stick-by-chip-heath-and-dan-heath/)
  argues for ideas that are simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional,
  and carried by stories. Generic AI copy tends to be abstract, expected, and
  unsupported.
- Simon Sinek's [*Start with
  Why*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/304046/start-with-why-15th-anniversary-edition-by-simon-sinek/)
  starts communication with purpose. A cookie-cutter deck often starts with a
  format and hopes the purpose appears later.
- Jonah Berger's [*Contagious*](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Contagious/Jonah-Berger/9781451686586)
  focuses on practical value, emotion, triggers, and stories. A polished email
  full of vague benefits gives people little reason to remember or share it.

</Callout>

The same defaults show up in prose, where repeated sentence patterns are easier
to spot and edit.

The same defaults show up in generated marketing emails. You get a purple
gradient, abstract circles, "Hi [First Name]," a sentence about an exciting
announcement, three equal benefit cards, and a rounded "Discover more" button.

The email is readable, but none of its copy or visual choices identify the
company behind it.

Brand is the set of choices that makes the content recognizable.
Marty Neumeier's influential [*The Brand
Gap*](https://www.martyneumeier.com/the-brand-gap) frames brand as the bridge
between strategy and the experience people actually have. That is why
changing the logo on an interchangeable artifact does not make it branded.
The same test works for a personal site: remove the name and avatar. Do the
ideas, examples, and visual choices still identify the author?

## Do not let AI skip the decisions

This prompt asks the model to invent the argument, evidence, story, hierarchy,
and visual system in one pass:

```text
Create a professional 12-slide presentation about our AI transformation
strategy. Use a modern design, compelling visuals, and an executive tone.
```

There is almost no information in it. "Professional," "modern," and
"compelling" describe a safe middle. The model has to supply everything else.

Designers describe the same failure from the other side of the prompt box. A
CHI 2025 study based on interviews and the participants' actual design
artifacts found recurring frustration with
["take it or leave it" generation, ignored specifics, and inconsistent
styles](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713233). Professional designers wanted
finer control and the ability to edit, not only another roll of the prompt
dice.

Before generation, define the audience, the claim you will defend, the evidence
behind it, and the brand rules the output must follow.

Use AI backstage:

- summarize research
- find gaps in the argument
- propose three narrative structures
- turn evidence into chart options
- generate variations within a chosen direction
- adapt approved material to another channel
- check a finished artifact against brand rules

A recent *Journal of Marketing* study found that
["off-the-shelf" visual generative AI was not aligned with marketing
objectives](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429251356993). The researchers had to
fine-tune a model on marketing metrics, adapt it to audiences, and incorporate
consumer feedback before generated ads could match or exceed conventional
ones.

AI visuals can work, but only after someone defines the audience and objective,
then evaluates the output against both.

## Build the brand context once

You should not explain your identity from scratch in every prompt. Put it in a
small, reusable source of truth.

```text
brand/
├── positioning.md
├── voice-and-language.md
├── design-tokens.json
├── proof-library/
│   ├── customer-stories.md
│   ├── metrics.csv
│   └── product-screenshots/
├── examples-we-like/
├── examples-we-reject/
└── templates/
    ├── executive-deck.potx
    ├── product-update.html
    └── social-card.svg
```

Keep the brief short. Focus on decisions.

```yaml
# brand/creative-brief.yml
audience:
  primary: engineering leaders at companies with 500+ developers
  tension: AI increased output, but review capacity did not grow

point_of_view:
  belief: generation is cheap; verification is the new bottleneck
  disagreement: more generated code is not the same as more delivered value

voice:
  use: [direct, technical, specific, slightly skeptical]
  avoid:
    - rapidly evolving landscape
    - unlock potential
    - seamless
    - empower
    - future-ready

evidence:
  require_source_for_numbers: true
  preferred: [product screenshots, real workflows, measured before-and-after]

visual:
  dominant: charcoal
  accent: electric coral
  type: large editorial headlines with compact technical labels
  motif: annotated system diagrams
  avoid: [purple gradients, equal pillar cards, stock people, floating orbs]
```

Tools can load this context automatically. GitHub Copilot supports
[repository-wide and path-specific custom
instructions](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/how-tos/copilot-on-github/customize-copilot/add-custom-instructions/add-repository-instructions),
so the same voice and content rules can travel with the project instead of
living in someone's prompt history.

For organizations, PowerPoint itself can carry the visual system. Microsoft
documents [brand templates for Copilot
Cowork](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/cowork/cowork-brand-templates)
that define fonts, colors, logos, and slide layouts. Teams can publish shared
templates and media through a SharePoint [organization assets
library](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sharepoint/organization-assets-library).

For AI-assisted work, a `.pptx` is not the whole template. Keep it beside the
custom instructions, reusable prompts or skills, Markdown references, approved
examples, and edge cases that govern generated content.

## Ask AI for options, preferably in `/plan` mode

Begin with a brief and ask for decisions you
can inspect.

```text
/plan Using the attached audience brief, evidence pack, and brand rules:

1. Propose three different narrative structures.
2. For each structure, state the main claim and the audience change it serves.
3. Map every factual claim to a source. Mark unsupported claims.
4. Suggest one visual metaphor that comes from the subject, not a template.
5. List the presentation clichés you intentionally avoided.
```

Choose a structure. Challenge it. Then ask for slide-level options:

```text
For slide 4, the audience needs to understand why review time rose after AI
adoption.

Propose three compositions:
- a queue diagram
- a before-and-after chart
- an annotated product screenshot

Use only the evidence in metrics.csv. No decorative cards. No invented
numbers. Explain what each composition makes easy to understand.
```

At that point, the model is working from decisions instead of inventing them.

## Personal brands need constraints too

For a personal site, write down:

1. **Three beliefs you return to.** These become the spine of your work.
2. **The proof you prefer.** Code, field notes, diagrams, benchmarks, or
   customer stories.
3. **Your language rules.** Sentence length, level of technical detail, words
   you use, and words you avoid.
4. **A visual motif.** Annotated screenshots, hand-drawn systems, terminal
   output, editorial type, or another format tied to your subject.
5. **Your rejection list.** Generic hooks, fake urgency, stock gradients,
   unsourced metrics, and anything else that does not sound or look like you.

Give those constraints to the model. Reject any draft that could have come from
someone else's account.

## Review the artifact before it reviews you

Use this five-question check before publishing a deck, email, landing page, or
post:

```text
[ ] Could a competitor publish this unchanged?
[ ] Is every number tied to a named source or measurement?
[ ] Does each section make one clear claim?
[ ] Is the hierarchy based on importance rather than symmetry?
[ ] Is there at least one idea, phrase, example, or visual only we would use?
```

If the first answer is yes, the work is not done.

AI can draft the deck. You still own the claim and the evidence behind it.
