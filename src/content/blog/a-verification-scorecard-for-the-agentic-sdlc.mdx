By 2026, code generation stopped being the hard part. Teams that lean on AI agents
merge [98% more pull requests](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering)
than teams that don't, and they finish more tasks doing it. The catch is that the
gains stay stuck at the individual level. In that same study of more than 10,000
developers, the extra output never showed up in company throughput, in DORA
metrics, or in quality.

It's a plumbing problem. An agent can open three or four PRs in the time it takes
to sit through standup, and every one of them still needs a person to decide
whether it's safe to ship. Nobody automated that step, so it's where the backlog now
collects. Google's DORA team calls the cost of it the "verification tax" in its
April 2026 [ROI of AI-Assisted Software Development](https://dora.dev/ai/roi/report/)
report, and its one-line summary of the shift is worth stealing: "We don't measure
AI by the code it writes but by the bottlenecks it clears."

This post takes that literally. It's a small verification scorecard you can
assemble from GitHub and CI data, and it ignores how much code you ship on
purpose. What it measures is how much it costs you to trust a change enough to
release it.

## What DORA measures

[DORA](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics/) tracks five metrics in two groups.
Throughput covers change lead time, deployment frequency, and failed deployment
recovery time. Instability covers change fail rate and deployment rework rate.
They're still a solid backbone, and DORA leans on them heavily in that April 2026
[ROI report](https://dora.dev/ai/roi/report/).

Deployment frequency and change lead time are the easiest to inflate once an agent
does the typing. Both can climb while the delivery you care about stays flat. That
is what Faros AI found. Its high-adoption teams merged 98% more PRs, and none of
that gain reached company throughput, DORA metrics, or quality.

| DORA signal | What it leaned on | Why it misleads in 2026 | What to watch instead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deployment frequency | Shipping was gated by human throughput | Agents open and merge changes at machine rate; the count inflates without more value | Share of changes that cleared a real verification bar |
| Change lead time | Commit to deploy tracked the expensive path | The authoring segment collapses toward pipeline runtime | Time to *verify*, not time to write and deploy |
| Change fail rate / rework | Lagging outcome of human-authored change | Still valid, but blind to how much unverified change you shipped | Keep it, and calibrate the bar against it |

## The bottleneck moved

The extra work lands in review, and the recent numbers agree on it from a few
directions at once:

- [Faros AI](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering) measured review
  time per PR up **91%** and average **PR size up 154%** on top of all those extra
  PRs, with bugs up roughly 9% per developer. The same reviewers now face more
  code in bigger diffs.
- LinearB's 2026 benchmarks, built from 8.1 million PRs, found that AI-generated
  code [waits 4.6x longer to get picked up for review](https://byteiota.com/software-engineering-benchmarks-2026-ai-code-review-gap/)
  than human code, and gets accepted only **32.7% of the time, against 84.4% for
  human-written code**. Review already accounts for about 57% of cycle time, and
  AI lengthens that queue rather than shortening it.
- Madrona's June 2026 [survey of engineering leaders](https://www.madrona.com/on-to-the-next-bottleneck-what-product-engineering-leaders-told-us-about-ai-in-software-development/)
  at organizations employing more than 10,000 engineers found the cost of
  generating software falling while, in their words, "the constraints are moving
  elsewhere." Asked where, **57% pointed at code review queue time**, and the main
  thing stopping them from giving agents more autonomy was their own capacity to
  validate the output.

This is just [Amdahl's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amdahl%27s_law). Make one
stage much faster, leave the next one alone, and the slow stage sets your ceiling.
Generation parallelizes across as many agents as you care to run. Verification
still comes down to a person reading a diff. DORA's April 2026 report even puts a
number on the damage, an "instability tax" where change failure rate creeps from
5% to 6% after adoption, and its advice is the boring kind that works: smaller
batches, automated testing, real CI.

## Change candidates

Agentic pipelines often skip the usual pull request. You get patch queues,
generated branches, merge-group commits, and bots pushing straight to a branch. To
cover all of them, I'll use the term **change candidate** instead
of PR. A candidate is a proposed diff at a specific revision, tagged with who wrote
it (human, agent, automation), where it's headed, how risky it is, which version
of the bar applies, what evidence it carries, and whether it shipped. A pull
request is one kind of candidate. Everything below is measured per candidate
revision.

## Write the bar down

Start with a written definition of what "verified" means. It has to scale with
risk, otherwise it becomes one more checklist people clear without lowering any
actual risk. A change counts as verified when the evidence behind it matches how
much damage it could do.

```yaml
# verification-bar.yml: evidence required scales with blast radius.
version: 3
tiers:
  - name: critical
    when:
      paths: ["auth/**", "billing/**", "db/migrations/**", "infra/**"]
    require:
      checks: [unit, integration, contract, sast, dast]
      changed_line_coverage: 0.90
      canary: { min_minutes: 30, healthy: true }
      human_approval: { count: 1, from: codeowners }
    bypass: { allowed: false }

  - name: standard
    when:
      paths: ["src/**"]
    require:
      checks: [unit, integration, sast]
      changed_line_coverage: 0.75
      human_approval: { count: 1 }
    bypass: { allowed: emergency, reason_required: true, expires_hours: 24, review_after: true }

  - name: low
    when:
      paths: ["docs/**", "**/*.md", "**/*.mdx"]
    require:
      checks: [lint]
    bypass: { allowed: true }
```

Version that file and review changes to it the way you would production code, and
record which version applied to each candidate at merge time, so later you can ask
which bar a given change actually cleared. It plays the same role as the repo
instructions file in
[Anatomy of an Agentic Coding Session](/blog/anatomy-of-an-agentic-coding-session/).
Agents do better work when "done" is written down in a form they can read.

## Mean Time to Verify (MTTV)

MTTV measures how long a candidate revision takes to go from created to cleared by
its bar. It isolates the part of change lead time agents didn't make cheaper,
mostly the wait on checks, review, fixes, and approvals.

```ts
import { Octokit } from "@octokit/rest";

// MTTV for a merged PR, approximated from the final reviewed head SHA.
// Real pipelines should emit candidate events; GitHub only gets you close.
async function mttvSeconds(octokit: Octokit, owner: string, repo: string, pull_number: number) {
  const { data: pr } = await octokit.pulls.get({ owner, repo, pull_number });
  const headSha = pr.merge_commit_sha ?? pr.head.sha;

  // t0: when the reviewed revision came into being (last commit on the head).
  const { data: commit } = await octokit.repos.getCommit({ owner, repo, ref: headSha });
  const proposedAt = new Date(commit.commit.committer?.date ?? pr.created_at);

  // t1a: last required check to go green on that revision.
  const { data: checks } = await octokit.checks.listForRef({ owner, repo, ref: headSha });
  const lastCheck = Math.max(
    ...checks.check_runs
      .filter((c) => c.conclusion === "success" && c.completed_at)
      .map((c) => new Date(c.completed_at!).getTime())
  );

  // t1b: last approving review.
  const { data: reviews } = await octokit.pulls.listReviews({ owner, repo, pull_number });
  const lastApproval = Math.max(
    ...reviews
      .filter((r) => r.state === "APPROVED" && r.submitted_at)
      .map((r) => new Date(r.submitted_at!).getTime())
  );

  const barCleared = Math.max(lastCheck, lastApproval); // both gates satisfied
  return (barCleared - proposedAt.getTime()) / 1000;
}
```

Report MTTV as percentiles (p50, p75, p95) rather than an average, and split the
total so you can see where the time actually goes. Wall-clock MTTV mixes real
verification work with idle waiting, and it helps to pull those apart:

- **Time to first check result**: CI and static-analysis latency.
- **Time to review decision**: human and domain approval latency.
- **Rework time**: from a failed check or review to the next candidate revision.
- **Queue time**: waiting on a person or a scarce environment.
- **Flow efficiency**: active verification time divided by total, the honest
  measure of how much of MTTV is real work versus waiting.

<Callout type="warning" title="GitHub will only get you close">
  MTTV needs an event model, and GitHub approximates it imperfectly. Squash
  merges change the head SHA, so the reviewed revision and the shipped commit
  differ. Merge queues run checks on a synthetic merge-group SHA, not the PR
  head. Approvals get dismissed by later commits. Branch protection and rulesets
  change over time, so "which bar did this clear" needs the bar as of merge time.
  Admin bypass is hard to reconstruct from check state alone. And agent diffs
  that never open a PR are invisible unless your platform emits a proposal event.
  Emit candidate events if you can; treat the GitHub-only version as a first
  approximation.
</Callout>

## Coverage, weighted by risk

Coverage measures how much of the risk you shipped actually cleared the bar. Count
every change equally and a thousand docs tweaks can hide one unreviewed auth
change, so weight each change by its risk. That gives you Risk-Adjusted
Verification Coverage.

```sql
-- change_events(candidate_sha, service, risk_weight, bar_cleared,
--               bypass_reason, deployed_at, caused_incident)
-- risk_weight: low=1, standard=3, critical=10 (tune per org).

-- Risk-Adjusted Verification Coverage over the last 30 days.
SELECT
  SUM(CASE WHEN bar_cleared THEN risk_weight ELSE 0 END)::float
    / NULLIF(SUM(risk_weight), 0) AS verification_coverage
FROM change_events
WHERE deployed_at >= now() - interval '30 days';

-- Calibration: does clearing the bar actually predict fewer incidents?
SELECT
  risk_weight,
  bar_cleared,
  COUNT(*)                                         AS changes,
  AVG(CASE WHEN caused_incident THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS incident_rate
FROM change_events
WHERE deployed_at >= now() - interval '90 days'
GROUP BY risk_weight, bar_cleared
ORDER BY risk_weight DESC, bar_cleared;
```

Until that calibration query shows the bar predicting fewer incidents, treat
coverage as a promising leading signal rather than a proven one. If your fully
verified critical changes fail about as often as the bypassed ones, the bar isn't
predicting real risk, and knowing that is worth more than another deploy count.

## Keep it honest

The obvious failure mode is trading velocity theater for verification theater,
where a dashboard reads "97% verified" while incidents keep landing. Keeping it
honest takes three habits.

Use the metrics together instead of one at a time. MTTV, coverage, and change fail
rate each catch a different kind of problem, and any one of them alone is easy to
game. That is the point of a scorecard, because any single delivery number gets
gamed the moment it becomes the target.

Calibrate the bar against production outcomes: fail rate and incident rate by tier,
rollback rate for agent-authored versus human-authored changes, changes that
cleared the full bar and still broke, and oversized requirements on low-risk
changes that bought you nothing. If a check has never caught a bug, it shouldn't
count as strong evidence.

Then watch the bypass path, since bypasses can make both MTTV and coverage look
better than they are.

| Metric | How it gets gamed | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MTTV | Open the PR only after the agent iterated privately; keep it draft until green; split risky work into tiny PRs | Measure from first proposal event; track candidate churn and failed attempts; report p95, not just p50 |
| Coverage | Weaken the bar; move risky checks out of "required"; abuse emergency bypass | Version and review the bar; require reason-coded, expiring bypass; alert on bypass by team and service |
| Both | Downgrade a change's risk tier to dodge evidence | Treat tier and bar changes as first-class governance events; audit them |

<Callout type="note" title="Confidence is not correctness">
  A change that clears a strong bar can still be wrong, the same way a
  high-probability token can still be false (see
  [A Case for Small Context Windows](/blog/a-case-for-small-context-windows/)).
  The scorecard tells you the process was followed. Whether the code is actually
  correct is a separate question, and the lagging outcomes are what answer it and
  keep the bar honest.
</Callout>

## Where to start

You can start without the full event pipeline. A stripped-down version runs on
data you already have:

1. Write a two or three tier `verification-bar.yml` and commit it.
2. Compute MTTV p50 and p95 from PR, check-run, and review timestamps.
3. Compute Risk-Adjusted Verification Coverage from merged PRs plus your deploy
   log, tagging bypasses.
4. Once a month, run the calibration query and tighten or loosen the bar where the
   tiers don't track incidents.

Two changes help right away, and the 2026 benchmark data shows the strongest teams
already make them. Cap PR size so a reviewer can hold the whole change in their
head, and spread review across the team so one senior engineer doesn't turn into
the bottleneck. Both cut the waiting time inside MTTV.

Keep tracking deployment frequency and lead time. They still describe the system,
they just shouldn't be the headline number anymore. The better questions are what
it costs you to trust a change, how much of your real risk cleared the bar, and
whether clearing it actually keeps incidents down. Or, in DORA's own words from
2026: measure the bottleneck the agent clears, not the code it writes.
