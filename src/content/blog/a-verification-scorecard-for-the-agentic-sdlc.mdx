Your delivery dashboard says the team got faster: more deploys, shorter lead
time. Now that an agent writes most of the diffs, that was almost guaranteed to
happen. The question the dashboard can no longer answer is whether faster meant
better.

There is real evidence the two have come apart. In a 2025 randomized trial,
[METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)
had 16 experienced open-source developers work 246 of their own issues with and
without AI. With AI they were **19% slower**, yet they expected a 24% speedup and
still believed they had gotten a 20% speedup after the fact
([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)). Perception and measurement pointed
in opposite directions. If the people doing the work can't feel the difference,
a throughput chart won't show it to you either.

This post is a practitioner's answer: a small verification scorecard you can wire
into GitHub and CI, built to measure trust to ship rather than volume of change.

## What DORA actually measures (and where agents strain it)

[DORA](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics/) is a five-metric model, split into
throughput (change lead time, deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery
time) and instability (change fail rate, deployment rework rate). These are good
metrics. They grade the whole delivery system, and DORA's own research keeps
finding that speed and stability rise together for strong teams.

The strain isn't that the numbers are wrong. It's that two of them, deployment
frequency and change lead time, were informative because human authoring used to
be a real cost. Remove that cost and inflate the change count, and those two
signals drift away from delivered value. DORA says as much in its own
[pitfalls](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics/): "Setting metrics as a goal.
Ignoring Goodhart's law." That is exactly the failure mode agents make easy.

| DORA signal | What it leaned on | Why it strains under agents | What to watch instead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deployment frequency | Shipping was gated by human throughput | Agents open and merge changes at machine rate; the count inflates without more value | Share of changes that cleared a real verification bar |
| Change lead time | Commit to deploy tracked the expensive path | The authoring segment collapses toward pipeline runtime | Time to *verify*, not time to write and deploy |
| Change fail rate / rework | Lagging outcome of human-authored change | Still valid, but blind to how much unverified change you shipped | Keep it, and calibrate the bar against it |

## Why the throughput signals wobble

The clean way to name this is Goodhart's law. Manheim and Garrabrant's
[taxonomy](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.04585) catalogs several mechanisms and
makes the key point directly: Goodhart effects scale with the "optimization
power" pointed at the proxy, which is why they call AI's added optimization power
"especially critical." A metric that a person could nudge is a metric an agent
can pin to the ceiling. Deployment frequency is the easiest to pin.

The evidence that volume has decoupled from quality is not subtle:

- **Churn is rising.** GitClear's analysis of ~153 million changed lines found
  code churn (lines reverted or reworked within two weeks) on pace to
  [double in 2024](https://www.gitclear.com/coding_on_copilot_data_shows_ais_downward_pressure_on_code_quality)
  versus its 2021 pre-AI baseline, with more copy-pasted code and less reuse.
- **Trust is low.** In DORA's
  [2024 survey](https://dora.dev/research/2024/ai-preview/), only 24% of
  developers said they trust AI-generated code "a lot" or "a great deal."
- **Security regresses.** In a controlled study, participants with an AI
  assistant wrote [significantly less secure code](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03622)
  (Perry et al., CCS 2023) and were more likely to believe it was secure. Copilot
  emitted a vulnerability in about
  [40% of 1,689 generated programs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.09293) across
  high-risk scenarios (Pearce et al., IEEE S&P 2022).

So the constraint moved. Theory of Constraints (Goldratt) has a blunt rule:
optimizing anything that isn't the bottleneck is waste. When authoring stops
being the bottleneck, driving authoring throughput harder just piles inventory in
front of the new bottleneck, which is establishing enough trust to ship. Review
is where that trust gets built, and review does not scale like generation. Review
effectiveness [drops as change size grows](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSE.2013.6606617)
(Bacchelli and Bird, ICSE 2013), and Google's guidance is to keep changes near
100 lines because large ones get
[reviewed less thoroughly](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html).
That same guide grants one exception for a big diff: when it was produced by "a
tool that you trust completely." An agent is precisely the tool whose output you
can't yet trust completely. That is the whole problem in one sentence.

## The unit is a change candidate, not a PR

Agentic pipelines don't always look like a person opening a pull request. They
produce patch queues, generated branches, merge-group commits, and direct
automation commits. To measure them uniformly, define the primitive as a **change
candidate**: a proposed diff at a specific revision, with an author type
(human, agent, automation), a target environment, a risk class, a verification
bar version, an evidence set, and a promotion decision. A GitHub PR is one
implementation of a candidate, not the definition. Everything below is computed
per candidate revision.

## The verification bar as code

The centerpiece is a declarative definition of what "verified" means, and it has
to be risk-adaptive or it becomes the exact compliance theater it's meant to
replace. A verified change is one whose required evidence matches the risk of the
surface it touched.

```yaml
# verification-bar.yml: evidence required scales with blast radius.
version: 3
tiers:
  - name: critical
    when:
      paths: ["auth/**", "billing/**", "db/migrations/**", "infra/**"]
    require:
      checks: [unit, integration, contract, sast, dast]
      changed_line_coverage: 0.90
      canary: { min_minutes: 30, healthy: true }
      human_approval: { count: 1, from: codeowners }
    bypass: { allowed: false }

  - name: standard
    when:
      paths: ["src/**"]
    require:
      checks: [unit, integration, sast]
      changed_line_coverage: 0.75
      human_approval: { count: 1 }
    bypass: { allowed: emergency, reason_required: true, expires_hours: 24, review_after: true }

  - name: low
    when:
      paths: ["docs/**", "**/*.md", "**/*.mdx"]
    require:
      checks: [lint]
    bypass: { allowed: true }
```

The bar is a real artifact: versioned, reviewed like production code, and stamped
onto every candidate at merge time (so you can later ask which bar a change
actually cleared). This is the same idea as the repo instructions file from
[Anatomy of an Agentic Coding Session](/blog/anatomy-of-an-agentic-coding-session/):
the highest-leverage thing you can hand an agent is a machine-readable definition
of done.

## Mean Time to Verify (MTTV)

MTTV is the elapsed time from a candidate revision's creation to the first moment
that same revision satisfies its bar. It's the successor to change lead time,
scoped to the segment that now costs something.

```ts
import { Octokit } from "@octokit/rest";

// MTTV for a merged PR, approximated from the final reviewed head SHA.
// Real pipelines should emit candidate events; GitHub only gets you close.
async function mttvSeconds(octokit: Octokit, owner: string, repo: string, pull_number: number) {
  const { data: pr } = await octokit.pulls.get({ owner, repo, pull_number });
  const headSha = pr.merge_commit_sha ?? pr.head.sha;

  // t0: when the reviewed revision came into being (last commit on the head).
  const { data: commit } = await octokit.repos.getCommit({ owner, repo, ref: headSha });
  const proposedAt = new Date(commit.commit.committer?.date ?? pr.created_at);

  // t1a: last required check to go green on that revision.
  const { data: checks } = await octokit.checks.listForRef({ owner, repo, ref: headSha });
  const lastCheck = Math.max(
    ...checks.check_runs
      .filter((c) => c.conclusion === "success" && c.completed_at)
      .map((c) => new Date(c.completed_at!).getTime())
  );

  // t1b: last approving review.
  const { data: reviews } = await octokit.pulls.listReviews({ owner, repo, pull_number });
  const lastApproval = Math.max(
    ...reviews
      .filter((r) => r.state === "APPROVED" && r.submitted_at)
      .map((r) => new Date(r.submitted_at!).getTime())
  );

  const barCleared = Math.max(lastCheck, lastApproval); // both gates satisfied
  return (barCleared - proposedAt.getTime()) / 1000;
}
```

Report MTTV as a distribution (p50, p75, p95), not a mean, and split it so you
optimize the right part. Wall-clock MTTV mixes active verification with waiting,
and Reinertsen's flow work (*The Principles of Product Development Flow*) is the
reason to separate them:

- **Time to first check result**: CI and static-analysis latency.
- **Time to review decision**: human and domain approval latency.
- **Rework time**: from a failed check or review to the next candidate revision.
- **Queue time**: waiting on a person or a scarce environment.
- **Flow efficiency**: active verification time divided by total, the honest
  measure of how much of MTTV is real work versus waiting.

<Callout type="warning" title="GitHub will only get you close">
  MTTV needs an event model, and GitHub approximates it imperfectly. Squash
  merges change the head SHA, so the reviewed revision and the shipped commit
  differ. Merge queues run checks on a synthetic merge-group SHA, not the PR
  head. Approvals get dismissed by later commits. Branch protection and rulesets
  change over time, so "which bar did this clear" needs the bar as of merge time.
  Admin bypass is hard to reconstruct from check state alone. And agent diffs
  that never open a PR are invisible unless your platform emits a proposal event.
  Emit candidate events if you can; treat the GitHub-only version as a first
  approximation.
</Callout>

## Risk-Adjusted Verification Coverage

Coverage answers the leading question: of the risk you shipped, how much actually
cleared the bar? Counting changes equally lets a thousand docs edits hide one
unverified auth change, so weight by risk.

```sql
-- change_events(candidate_sha, service, risk_weight, bar_cleared,
--               bypass_reason, deployed_at, caused_incident)
-- risk_weight: low=1, standard=3, critical=10 (tune per org).

-- Risk-Adjusted Verification Coverage over the last 30 days.
SELECT
  SUM(CASE WHEN bar_cleared THEN risk_weight ELSE 0 END)::float
    / NULLIF(SUM(risk_weight), 0) AS verification_coverage
FROM change_events
WHERE deployed_at >= now() - interval '30 days';

-- Calibration: does clearing the bar actually predict fewer incidents?
SELECT
  risk_weight,
  bar_cleared,
  COUNT(*)                                         AS changes,
  AVG(CASE WHEN caused_incident THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS incident_rate
FROM change_events
WHERE deployed_at >= now() - interval '90 days'
GROUP BY risk_weight, bar_cleared
ORDER BY risk_weight DESC, bar_cleared;
```

Coverage is a leading exposure *candidate*, not proof of safety, until that second
query earns it. If fully verified critical changes have the same incident rate as
bypassed ones, your bar is decorative and you've learned something more useful
than any deploy count.

## Calibrate it, or it's just verification theater

The honest risk here is real: you can swap velocity theater for verification
theater, where "97% verified" sits next to a steady stream of incidents. Three
things keep the scorecard honest.

First, **read it as a constellation in tension**, the way the
[SPACE framework](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3454122.3454124) (Forsgren et
al., 2021) argues productivity must be read. MTTV, coverage, and change fail rate
constrain each other; any one alone is gameable.

Second, **calibrate the bar against outcomes**: change fail rate and incident
rate by verification tier, rollback rate for agent- versus human-authored
changes, "false confidence" (cleared the full bar and still caused an incident),
and "excess friction" (heavy bar on low-risk changes with no payoff). A green
check that never catches a defect should stop counting as evidence.

Third, **govern the bypass path**, because that's where both metrics get gamed:

| Metric | How it gets gamed | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MTTV | Open the PR only after the agent iterated privately; keep it draft until green; split risky work into tiny PRs | Measure from first proposal event; track candidate churn and failed attempts; report p95, not just p50 |
| Coverage | Weaken the bar; move risky checks out of "required"; abuse emergency bypass | Version and review the bar; require reason-coded, expiring bypass; alert on bypass by team and service |
| Both | Downgrade a change's risk tier to dodge evidence | Treat tier and bar changes as first-class governance events; audit them |

<Callout type="note" title="Confidence is not correctness">
  A change that clears a strong bar can still be wrong, the same way a
  high-probability token can still be false (see
  [A Case for Small Context Windows](/blog/a-case-for-small-context-windows/)).
  The scorecard measures process trust, not truth. That's why the lagging
  outcomes stay in the picture: they're the ground truth the bar is calibrated
  against.
</Callout>

## Adopt it Monday

You don't need the full event model to start. The minimal version runs on data
you already have:

1. Write a two or three tier `verification-bar.yml` and commit it.
2. Compute MTTV p50 and p95 from PR, check-run, and review timestamps.
3. Compute Risk-Adjusted Verification Coverage from merged PRs plus your deploy
   log, tagging bypasses.
4. Once a month, run the calibration query and adjust the bar where the tiers
   don't predict incidents.

Keep deployment frequency and lead time on the dashboard. They still describe the
system. Just stop treating them as the score. When writing code is cheap, the
scarce resource is trust, so measure the cost of earning it (MTTV), the coverage
of it (Risk-Adjusted Verification Coverage), and whether it actually predicts
safety (calibration). That's the number worth moving.
