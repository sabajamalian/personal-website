By 2026 the code part got fast. Teams leaning on AI agents merge
[98% more pull requests](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering) and
finish more tasks than the teams that don't, and the generation problem is
roughly solved. What almost nobody can show is that any of it made delivery
better. In that same study of more than 10,000 developers, the team-level gains
don't correlate with company-level throughput, DORA metrics, or quality. The
individual wins just don't add up to an organizational one.

They get absorbed somewhere, and it isn't a mystery where. When an agent opens
three or four PRs before you've finished standup, the work doesn't vanish, it
slides downstream to the one step nobody automated: deciding whether the change
is safe to ship. Google's DORA team has a name for this in its April 2026
[ROI of AI-Assisted Software Development](https://dora.dev/ai/roi/report/) report,
the "verification tax," and a good line about what to do with it: "We don't
measure AI by the code it writes but by the bottlenecks it clears."

That's what this post is about. What follows is a small verification scorecard you
can build from GitHub and CI data. It ignores how much code you're shipping and
watches the thing that actually went scarce: the capacity to trust a change enough
to release it.

## What DORA measures

[DORA](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics/) is five metrics in two groups.
Throughput is change lead time, deployment frequency, and failed deployment
recovery time. Instability is change fail rate and deployment rework rate.
They're still the right backbone, and DORA hasn't abandoned them; its April 2026
[ROI report](https://dora.dev/ai/roi/report/) leans on them heavily.

The trouble is that two of them, deployment frequency and change lead time, get
easy to run up once an agent is doing the typing. You can post big gains on both
while the delivery you actually care about goes nowhere. That's not a hunch: in
[Faros AI's study](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering) of 10,000-plus
developers, high-adoption teams merged 98% more PRs, and none of that team-level
lift showed up in company throughput, DORA metrics, or quality. The dashboard
number went up. The outcome didn't.

| DORA signal | What it leaned on | Why it misleads in 2026 | What to watch instead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deployment frequency | Shipping was gated by human throughput | Agents open and merge changes at machine rate; the count inflates without more value | Share of changes that cleared a real verification bar |
| Change lead time | Commit to deploy tracked the expensive path | The authoring segment collapses toward pipeline runtime | Time to *verify*, not time to write and deploy |
| Change fail rate / rework | Lagging outcome of human-authored change | Still valid, but blind to how much unverified change you shipped | Keep it, and calibrate the bar against it |

## The bottleneck moved

So where did the work go? To review. This is the most consistent finding in the
recent data, and it shows up from several directions at once:

- Alongside those extra PRs,
  [Faros AI](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering) measured review
  time per PR up **91%**, average **PR size up 154%**, and bugs up about 9% per
  developer. More code, in bigger chunks, landing on the same reviewers.
- LinearB's 2026 benchmarks, drawn from 8.1 million PRs, found AI-generated code
  [waits 4.6x longer to get picked up for review](https://byteiota.com/software-engineering-benchmarks-2026-ai-code-review-gap/)
  than human code, and clears at an acceptance rate of just **32.7%, against
  84.4% for human-written code**. Review already eats roughly 57% of cycle time,
  and AI widens that queue rather than shrinking it.
- Madrona's June 2026
  [survey of engineering leaders](https://www.madrona.com/on-to-the-next-bottleneck-what-product-engineering-leaders-told-us-about-ai-in-software-development/)
  (organizations employing 10,000-plus engineers) said it plainly: the cost of
  generating software is dropping and "the constraints are moving elsewhere."
  Asked where the bottleneck went, **57% named code review queue time**; the top
  thing holding them back from giving agents more autonomy was capacity to
  validate the output.

None of this is exotic. It's [Amdahl's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amdahl%27s_law):
speed up one stage all you want, and the stage you didn't speed up sets the
ceiling. Generation was the parallel part. Verification is the serial part, and
it's still mostly a human reading a diff. DORA's April 2026 report models the cost
directly as an "instability tax," with change failure rate drifting from 5% to 6%
after adoption, and prescribes the unglamorous fixes: smaller batches, automated
testing, real CI.

## Change candidates

Agentic pipelines don't always route through a person opening a pull request. You
get patch queues, generated branches, merge-group commits, bots pushing straight
to a branch. To measure all of it the same way, I'll talk about a **change
candidate** rather than a PR: a proposed diff at a specific revision, tagged with
who wrote it (human, agent, automation), where it's headed, how risky it is, which
version of the bar applies to it, what evidence it carries, and whether it got
promoted. A pull request is just one shape a candidate takes. Everything below is
per candidate revision.

## Write the bar down

Everything else hangs off a written definition of what "verified" means, and it
has to scale with risk or it turns into the box-ticking it's meant to replace. A
change is verified when the evidence behind it matches how much damage it could
do.

```yaml
# verification-bar.yml: evidence required scales with blast radius.
version: 3
tiers:
  - name: critical
    when:
      paths: ["auth/**", "billing/**", "db/migrations/**", "infra/**"]
    require:
      checks: [unit, integration, contract, sast, dast]
      changed_line_coverage: 0.90
      canary: { min_minutes: 30, healthy: true }
      human_approval: { count: 1, from: codeowners }
    bypass: { allowed: false }

  - name: standard
    when:
      paths: ["src/**"]
    require:
      checks: [unit, integration, sast]
      changed_line_coverage: 0.75
      human_approval: { count: 1 }
    bypass: { allowed: emergency, reason_required: true, expires_hours: 24, review_after: true }

  - name: low
    when:
      paths: ["docs/**", "**/*.md", "**/*.mdx"]
    require:
      checks: [lint]
    bypass: { allowed: true }
```

Treat that file like production code: version it, review changes to it, and stamp
the version onto each candidate at merge time so you can go back later and ask
which bar a given change actually cleared. It's the same move as the repo
instructions file in
[Anatomy of an Agentic Coding Session](/blog/anatomy-of-an-agentic-coding-session/).
The most useful thing you can hand an agent is a machine-readable definition of
done.

## Mean Time to Verify (MTTV)

MTTV is how long a candidate revision sits between coming into existence and
clearing its bar. Think of it as the slice of change lead time that still costs
you something.

```ts
import { Octokit } from "@octokit/rest";

// MTTV for a merged PR, approximated from the final reviewed head SHA.
// Real pipelines should emit candidate events; GitHub only gets you close.
async function mttvSeconds(octokit: Octokit, owner: string, repo: string, pull_number: number) {
  const { data: pr } = await octokit.pulls.get({ owner, repo, pull_number });
  const headSha = pr.merge_commit_sha ?? pr.head.sha;

  // t0: when the reviewed revision came into being (last commit on the head).
  const { data: commit } = await octokit.repos.getCommit({ owner, repo, ref: headSha });
  const proposedAt = new Date(commit.commit.committer?.date ?? pr.created_at);

  // t1a: last required check to go green on that revision.
  const { data: checks } = await octokit.checks.listForRef({ owner, repo, ref: headSha });
  const lastCheck = Math.max(
    ...checks.check_runs
      .filter((c) => c.conclusion === "success" && c.completed_at)
      .map((c) => new Date(c.completed_at!).getTime())
  );

  // t1b: last approving review.
  const { data: reviews } = await octokit.pulls.listReviews({ owner, repo, pull_number });
  const lastApproval = Math.max(
    ...reviews
      .filter((r) => r.state === "APPROVED" && r.submitted_at)
      .map((r) => new Date(r.submitted_at!).getTime())
  );

  const barCleared = Math.max(lastCheck, lastApproval); // both gates satisfied
  return (barCleared - proposedAt.getTime()) / 1000;
}
```

Report it as a spread (p50, p75, p95) rather than an average, and break it apart
so you know what you're actually fixing. Wall-clock MTTV lumps real verification
work in with plain waiting, and those are worth separating:

- **Time to first check result**: CI and static-analysis latency.
- **Time to review decision**: human and domain approval latency.
- **Rework time**: from a failed check or review to the next candidate revision.
- **Queue time**: waiting on a person or a scarce environment.
- **Flow efficiency**: active verification time divided by total, the honest
  measure of how much of MTTV is real work versus waiting.

<Callout type="warning" title="GitHub will only get you close">
  MTTV needs an event model, and GitHub approximates it imperfectly. Squash
  merges change the head SHA, so the reviewed revision and the shipped commit
  differ. Merge queues run checks on a synthetic merge-group SHA, not the PR
  head. Approvals get dismissed by later commits. Branch protection and rulesets
  change over time, so "which bar did this clear" needs the bar as of merge time.
  Admin bypass is hard to reconstruct from check state alone. And agent diffs
  that never open a PR are invisible unless your platform emits a proposal event.
  Emit candidate events if you can; treat the GitHub-only version as a first
  approximation.
</Callout>

## Coverage, weighted by risk

Coverage asks a different question: of all the risk you shipped, how much of it
cleared the bar? Count every change the same and a thousand docs tweaks will
cheerfully bury one unreviewed auth change, so weight it by risk. Call that
Risk-Adjusted Verification Coverage.

```sql
-- change_events(candidate_sha, service, risk_weight, bar_cleared,
--               bypass_reason, deployed_at, caused_incident)
-- risk_weight: low=1, standard=3, critical=10 (tune per org).

-- Risk-Adjusted Verification Coverage over the last 30 days.
SELECT
  SUM(CASE WHEN bar_cleared THEN risk_weight ELSE 0 END)::float
    / NULLIF(SUM(risk_weight), 0) AS verification_coverage
FROM change_events
WHERE deployed_at >= now() - interval '30 days';

-- Calibration: does clearing the bar actually predict fewer incidents?
SELECT
  risk_weight,
  bar_cleared,
  COUNT(*)                                         AS changes,
  AVG(CASE WHEN caused_incident THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS incident_rate
FROM change_events
WHERE deployed_at >= now() - interval '90 days'
GROUP BY risk_weight, bar_cleared
ORDER BY risk_weight DESC, bar_cleared;
```

Until that second query pays off, coverage is a candidate for a leading signal
and nothing more. If your fully verified critical changes fall over about as often
as the bypassed ones, the bar is decoration, which is itself worth knowing far
more than another deploy count is.

## Keep it honest

There's an obvious way for this to go wrong: you drop velocity theater and pick up
verification theater, where "97% verified" glows on a dashboard next to a steady
drip of incidents. A few habits keep that from happening.

Read the scorecard as a set of numbers that check each other. MTTV, coverage, and
change fail rate pull in different directions, and any single one on its own is
easy to fake. No delivery number survives being turned into a target, which is the
whole reason this is a scorecard and not one more hero metric.

Then calibrate the bar against what actually happens: fail rate and incident rate
per tier, rollback rate for agent-authored versus human-authored changes, the
changes that cleared the whole bar and still fell over, the heavy bars you slapped
on low-risk changes for no reason. A check that has never once caught a bug isn't
evidence, so stop treating it like it is.

And keep an eye on the bypass path, because that's where both numbers get gamed:

| Metric | How it gets gamed | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MTTV | Open the PR only after the agent iterated privately; keep it draft until green; split risky work into tiny PRs | Measure from first proposal event; track candidate churn and failed attempts; report p95, not just p50 |
| Coverage | Weaken the bar; move risky checks out of "required"; abuse emergency bypass | Version and review the bar; require reason-coded, expiring bypass; alert on bypass by team and service |
| Both | Downgrade a change's risk tier to dodge evidence | Treat tier and bar changes as first-class governance events; audit them |

<Callout type="note" title="Confidence is not correctness">
  A change that clears a strong bar can still be wrong, the same way a
  high-probability token can still be false (see
  [A Case for Small Context Windows](/blog/a-case-for-small-context-windows/)).
  The scorecard tells you the process was followed; it can't tell you the code is
  correct. That's what the lagging outcomes are for: they're the ground truth the
  bar gets calibrated against.
</Callout>

## Where to start

You don't need the whole event pipeline to get going. A stripped-down version
runs on data you already have:

1. Write a two or three tier `verification-bar.yml` and commit it.
2. Compute MTTV p50 and p95 from PR, check-run, and review timestamps.
3. Compute Risk-Adjusted Verification Coverage from merged PRs plus your deploy
   log, tagging bypasses.
4. Once a month, run the calibration query and adjust the bar where the tiers
   don't predict incidents.

Two things are worth doing in parallel, because the 2026 benchmark data shows
elite teams already do them: cap PR size so reviews stay reviewable, and spread
the review load so no single senior engineer becomes the queue. Both pull straight
on MTTV.

Leave deployment frequency and lead time up on the wall. They still say something
real about the system. They just shouldn't be the score anymore. The score is
closer to a question: what does it cost us to trust a change, how much of our
actual risk is covered, and does clearing the bar keep us out of incidents. Or, to
borrow the line from DORA's own 2026 report: measure the bottleneck the agent
clears, not the code it writes.
